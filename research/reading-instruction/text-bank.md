# Text Bank

Candidate passages for readJson lessons. Screened against the material rule: human-written, free to
use, modern unless the grade is 11-12, fiction and nonfiction both.

Readability is Flesch-Kincaid grade level computed on the excerpt named below, not the whole page.
Complexity is the 0-3 rubric from `reading-instruction-design` (L = language, I = idea, S =
structure, K = knowledge). The Band code is the routing slot; grade target is the reader label.

## TB-001 — Why Do Leaves Change Colors in the Fall

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | U.S. National Park Service |
| URL | https://www.nps.gov/articles/why-do-leaves-change-in-the-fall.htm |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, explanatory |
| Topic domain | science and nature |
| Grade target | 7 |
| Band | Band 7.1 |
| Length | 192 words |
| FK grade | 7.81 |
| Complexity | L2, I2, S1, K1 |
| Best lesson fit | `main_idea_band_7_1_0001` |

**Central idea.** Trees drop their leaves and break down chlorophyll to save energy for winter,
which reveals the other pigments already in the leaf.

**Supporting details.**

- Chlorophyll absorbs sunlight to make food and gives leaves their green color.
- Shorter days and colder temperatures signal the tree to conserve energy.
- The tree sends an enzyme to break down chlorophyll.
- Other pigments become visible once chlorophyll is gone.
- Rain and cool weather affect how bright the colors are.
- The tree pulls remaining nutrients back into its branches before the leaf falls.

**Why it works.** The causal chain is explicit, every support is stated, and the passage is short
enough for two parts. The topic is adult enough for older readers without being technical.

**Cautions.** The source uses exclamation points; quote the passage as written or trim those
sentences. Define `pigment`, `chlorophyll`, and `enzyme` in context if the lesson asks about them.

## TB-002 — Where is Earth's Water?

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | U.S. Geological Survey, Water Science School |
| URL | https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/where-earths-water |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, explanatory |
| Topic domain | science and nature |
| Grade target | 7 |
| Band | Band 7.1 |
| Length | 174-word excerpt |
| FK grade | 8.29 |
| Complexity | L2, I2, S1, K1 |
| Best lesson fit | `main_idea_band_7_1_0002` (cell owner; may supply more of Band 7.1 main_idea if it yields 8 disjoint prose passages) |

**Central idea.** Almost all of Earth's water is salt water or locked in ice and underground, so
only a tiny share is fresh surface water that people can use.

**Supporting details.**

- About 96 percent of Earth's water is saline.
- Over 68 percent of freshwater is in ice and glaciers.
- About 30 percent of freshwater is underground.
- Surface freshwater is about 1/150th of one percent of total water.
- Rivers and lakes supply most of the water people use.

**Why it works.** The proportions create a clear main idea and a natural supporting-detail set. It
also supports a text-structure lesson on comparison and proportion.

**Cautions.** The full page is table-heavy. Use the prose excerpt, not the data table. The numbers
need to be checked against the page at authoring time.

## TB-004 — The Water in You: Water and the Human Body

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | U.S. Geological Survey, Water Science School |
| URL | https://www.usgs.gov/special-topics/water-science-school/science/water-you-water-and-human-body |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, explanatory |
| Topic domain | science |
| Grade target | 4 |
| Band | Band 4.1 (provisional slot `inference_band_4_1_0001`) |
| Length | 1,371 words total; the prose sections are usable |
| FK grade | 6.6 on the opening sections; 9.8 for the whole page |
| Complexity | L2, I2, S2, K1 |

**Central idea.** The share of water in the human body changes with age and body fat, and water is
required for nearly every function the body performs.

**Supporting details.** Babies are born about 78 percent water and fall to about 65 percent by age
one. Adult men are about 60 percent water, adult women about 55 percent, because fat tissue holds
less water than lean tissue. The brain and heart are about 73 percent water and the lungs about 83
percent. Even bones are about 31 percent water. Water carries nutrients, removes waste, and controls
body temperature.

**Why it works.** Two facts ("fat holds less water" and "women carry more fat") combine into an
unstated conclusion, which is the inference the lesson teaches. The percentages are concrete and the
scene is the reader's own body.

**Cautions.** The page mixes simple prose with percent tables and a 1945 citation. Excerpt only the
prose; the tables raise the score without adding inference work.

## TB-005 — What is a rip current?

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | NOAA National Ocean Service |
| URL | https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/ripcurrent.html |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, explanatory/safety |
| Topic domain | nature |
| Grade target | 4 |
| Band | Band 4.1 (provisional slot `inference_band_4_1_0002`) |
| Length | 209 words |
| FK grade | 7.4 |
| Complexity | L2, I2, S1, K1 |

**Central idea.** A rip current is a fast, narrow flow moving away from shore, and swimmers get into
trouble by fighting it instead of swimming parallel to the beach.

**Supporting details.** Rip currents run along the East, Gulf, and West coasts and in the Great
Lakes. They move up to eight feet per second, faster than an Olympic swimmer. A rip current is not a
rip tide. The way out is to swim parallel to shore.

**Why it works.** The safety rule is stated but its reason is not: the reader has to connect "faster
than an Olympic swimmer" to "do not swim against it." That is a clean two-fact join with real stakes.

**Cautions.** Very short, 209 words. It supports one lesson of two parts, not a cell. One paragraph
scores 14.0 on its own because of words like *prevalent* and *channels*; do not build an item on that
sentence alone.

## TB-006 — Dehydration

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | MedlinePlus, U.S. National Library of Medicine |
| URL | https://medlineplus.gov/dehydration.html |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, health explainer |
| Topic domain | science |
| Grade target | 4 |
| Band | Band 4.1 (provisional slot `inference_band_4_1_0003`) |
| Length | 396 words |
| FK grade | 8.5 page-level; the definition paragraphs measure about 5 |
| Complexity | L2, I2, S2, K1 |

**Central idea.** Dehydration happens when a person loses more fluid than they take in, and some
people are at higher risk because their bodies signal thirst weakly or lose fluid faster.

**Supporting details.** Dehydration is a loss of too much fluid from the body. It happens when fluid
loss exceeds intake. Some older adults lose their sense of thirst as they age. Infants and young
children dehydrate faster through diarrhea or vomiting. Some medicines and illnesses increase
urination or sweating.

**Why it works.** "Older adults lose their sense of thirst" plus "dehydration means not enough fluid"
produces the unstated conclusion: they can be dehydrated without feeling thirsty. That is exactly
the kind of clue-join the band should practice.

**Cautions.** Strip the site banner and the terminology notice before banking a passage. The at-risk
list names diabetes, cystic fibrosis, and kidney problems, which is outside grade-4 knowledge.

## TB-007 — Caves and Aquifers

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | U.S. National Park Service |
| URL | https://www.nps.gov/articles/caves-and-aquifers.htm |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, explanatory |
| Topic domain | nature |
| Grade target | 4 |
| Band | Band 4.1 (provisional slot `inference_band_4_1_0004`) |
| Length | 460 words |
| FK grade | 7.7 at the densest paragraph, 4.5 at the simplest |
| Complexity | L3, I1, S2, K1 |
| Placement | Placed one band below its language score under 1.2.1: idea load is one-step |

**Central idea.** Caves form when slightly acidic rainwater slowly dissolves bedrock, and the same
openings can hold groundwater like a sponge.

**Supporting details.** Rain picks up carbon dioxide from the air and soil, which makes it slightly
acidic. Slightly acidic water dissolves bedrock over a long time, which opens caves. Porous rock acts
like a sponge and stores groundwater. The openings in cave country hold a large share of the water
people use.

**Why it works.** Nothing in the passage requires background knowledge of chemistry: the reader
follows "rain picks up gas" to "water is slightly acidic" to "rock dissolves." At the same time the
topic is one people actually wonder about, which is the point of 1.2.1.

**Cautions.** `karst`, `porosity`, `aquifer`, and `soluble` are above the band and each must be
supported in `help[0]` or `tell`. The page's light voice ("Don't worry though!") stays out of student
copy.

## TB-008 — What Is a Black Hole? (Grades 5-8)

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | NASA |
| URL | https://www.nasa.gov/learning-resources/for-kids-and-students/what-is-a-black-hole-grades-5-8/ |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, explanatory |
| Topic domain | science |
| Grade target | 4 |
| Band | Band 4.1 (provisional slot `inference_band_4_1_0005`) |
| Length | ~900 words; use the mass-and-gravity sections |
| FK grade | 5.1 and 6.7 on the sections to be used; 9+ on the dense ones |
| Complexity | L2, I2, S2, K1 |
| Placement | Uses the mass-and-gravity sections under 1.2.1; the singularity material is skipped |

**Central idea.** A black hole pulls with the gravity of whatever mass it holds, so one with the
sun's mass would keep the planets in their orbits, and the sun itself is too small to become one.

**Supporting details.** A black hole with the sun's mass would have the sun's gravity. Earth would
not fall in; the planets would orbit it the same way. The sun lacks the mass to collapse into a black
hole. At the end of its life it will become a red giant and throw off its outer layers.

**Why it works.** The common wrong idea — that a black hole is a vacuum cleaner — dies on one
sentence: same mass means same gravity. That is a real misconception to build a distractor on, and
the topic is the most interesting thing in the bank.

**Cautions.** The page is written for students, so its framing is school-facing; the tutor must not
adopt a classroom voice. Skip the singularity and event-horizon paragraphs entirely.

## TB-009 — The Story of the Beaver Marsh

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | U.S. National Park Service |
| URL | https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/story-of-the-beaver-marsh.htm |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, narrative history of a place |

| Grade target | 4 |

| FK grade | 5.85 on the three passages together |
| Complexity | L2, I2, S2, K1 |
| Best lesson fit | `main_idea_band_4_2_0001` |

**Central idea.** A wetland that was drained and junked came back after volunteers cleared the junk
and beavers raised the water.

**Supporting details.** Land development and the Ohio and Erie Canal drained the wetland. A dairy
farm, homes, and an auto repair shop stood on the land by the 1970s. Beavers returned in the late

vivid, and not the idea.

**Cautions.** Local names (Lock 26, Riverview Road, Howe Meadow) were kept because they date the
place without adding difficulty. Recheck both percentages at authoring time.

**Central idea.** Water is always moving between the places it is stored, and people change that

**Supporting details.** Water is stored in the air, on the land, and below the ground, as liquid,

## TB-012 — Wildland Fire: What is a Prescribed Fire?

| Best lesson fit | `main_idea_band_4_2_0004` |

**Central idea.** A prescribed fire is a planned burn held to written conditions and used to reach a
goal on the land.

**Supporting details.** A prescription covers public and crew safety, weather, and the chance of
meeting the goal. Burns clear fuel near homes, create habitat, help rare species, and keep historic

**Central idea.** A hurricane is a storm fed by warm ocean water, and storms are sorted by wind speed

## TB-014 — What is a planet?

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | National Aeronautics and Space Administration |
| URL | https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/planets/what-is-a-planet/ |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, explanatory |

| Grade target | 4 |
| Band | Band 4.2 |
| Length | 308 words (three passages, 101-105 each) |
| FK grade | 5.69 on the three passages together |
| Complexity | L2, I3, S2, K2 |
| Best lesson fit | `main_idea_band_4_2_0006` |

**Central idea.** The word planet means what astronomers agree it means, and that agreement changed
when new objects were found.

**Supporting details.** The 2006 definition requires a planet to orbit a star, be round from its own

**Cautions.** Keep the disagreement, or the passage teaches a false certainty. The quoted IAU

## Rules for adding a text

1. Verify the license before adding it.
2. Name the exact excerpt and compute FK on that excerpt.
3. Score L/I/S/K and record the highest demand.
4. Write the central idea and three to five supporting details.
5. Record the lesson slot it fits, not just the topic.
6. Add a caution line. Every text has one.
7. **One passage per lesson; a work serves one cell.** Where a work can carry a cell, milk it: take
   every lesson's passage from it, as long as no displayed passage repeats and each lesson's passage
   is self-sufficient. A cell drawing on several works is a sourcing choice, not a defect
   (`DESIGN_RULES` 2.2). Record which cell a work serves, and how many disjoint passages it can
   yield (eight of 100-200 words, even if only four are authored now).
8. **Rotate the subject across a rung.** Record `topic_domain` on every row, and list the six
   domains of a rung before sourcing: no two consecutive lessons may share one, and a rung spans at
   least four. Federal science pages are the cheapest licence, not the default subject; a rung of six
   of them fails this rule even with clean licences.
9. **A work never serves two cells.** Not two bands, not two categories. The move from Band 5.1 to
   Band 6.1 is the transition a student always notices, so it gets fresh material.
