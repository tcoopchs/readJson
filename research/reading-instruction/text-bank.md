# Text Bank

Candidate passages for readJson lessons. Screened against the material rule: human-written, free to
use, modern unless the grade is 11-12, fiction and nonfiction both.

Readability is Flesch-Kincaid grade level computed on the excerpt named below, not the whole page.
Complexity is the 0-3 rubric from `reading-instruction-design` (L = language, I = idea, S =
structure, K = knowledge). The Band code is the routing slot; grade target is the reader label.

## TB-001 — Why Do Leaves Change Colors in the Fall

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | U.S. National Park Service |
| URL | https://www.nps.gov/articles/why-do-leaves-change-in-the-fall.htm |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, explanatory |
| Grade target | 7 |
| Band | Band 7.1 |
| Length | 192 words |
| FK grade | 7.81 |
| Complexity | L2, I2, S1, K1 |
| Best lesson fit | `main_idea_band_7_1_0001` |

**Central idea.** Trees drop their leaves and break down chlorophyll to save energy for winter,
which reveals the other pigments already in the leaf.

**Supporting details.**

- Chlorophyll absorbs sunlight to make food and gives leaves their green color.
- Shorter days and colder temperatures signal the tree to conserve energy.
- The tree sends an enzyme to break down chlorophyll.
- Other pigments become visible once chlorophyll is gone.
- Rain and cool weather affect how bright the colors are.
- The tree pulls remaining nutrients back into its branches before the leaf falls.

**Why it works.** The causal chain is explicit, every support is stated, and the passage is short
enough for two parts. The topic is adult enough for older readers without being technical.

**Cautions.** The source uses exclamation points; quote the passage as written or trim those
sentences. Define `pigment`, `chlorophyll`, and `enzyme` in context if the lesson asks about them.

## TB-002 — Where is Earth's Water?

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | U.S. Geological Survey, Water Science School |
| URL | https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/where-earths-water |
| License | U.S. federal government work; public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Nonfiction, explanatory |
| Grade target | 7 |
| Band | Band 7.1 |
| Length | 174-word excerpt |
| FK grade | 8.29 |
| Complexity | L2, I2, S1, K1 |
| Best lesson fit | `main_idea_band_7_1_0002` (cell owner; may supply more of Band 7.1 main_idea if it yields 8 disjoint prose passages) |

**Central idea.** Almost all of Earth's water is salt water or locked in ice and underground, so
only a tiny share is fresh surface water that people can use.

**Supporting details.**

- About 96 percent of Earth's water is saline.
- Over 68 percent of freshwater is in ice and glaciers.
- About 30 percent of freshwater is underground.
- Surface freshwater is about 1/150th of one percent of total water.
- Rivers and lakes supply most of the water people use.

**Why it works.** The proportions create a clear main idea and a natural supporting-detail set. It
also supports a text-structure lesson on comparison and proportion.

**Cautions.** The full page is table-heavy. Use the prose excerpt, not the data table. The numbers
need to be checked against the page at authoring time.

## TB-003 — The Diamond Necklace

| Field | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Source | Guy de Maupassant, 1884; Project Gutenberg |
| URL | https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3080 (Volume 04) |
| Clean text | https://viva.pressbooks.pub/compreader/chapter/necklace/ |
| License | Public domain in the U.S. |
| Genre | Fiction, short story |
| Grade target | 7 readability; 8-9 literary |
| Band | Band 9.1 for a literary lesson |
| Length | Full short story; an excerpt is required for one lesson |
| FK grade | Gutenberg reading ease 78.6, about 7th grade |
| Complexity | L2-3, I2, S2, K1-2 |
| Best lesson fit | `literary_text_band_9_1_0001`, not `main_idea_band_7_1_0001` |

**Central idea.** A young woman's desire for a richer life leads her to borrow a necklace, lose
it, and spend years paying for a replacement that was never valuable.

**Supporting details.**

- Mathilde is unhappy with her ordinary life and wants to appear wealthy.
- She borrows a necklace for the ball and loses it.
- The couple borrows heavily to buy a replacement.
- Years of hard work and poverty follow.
- The ending reveals the original necklace was paste.

**Why it is valuable.** Strong theme, irony, character change, and a clear narrative structure.
Useful for `literary_text` and `authors_craft`.

**Cautions.** It is 1884 and translated, so it violates the modern-text default below grade 11. The
full story is too long for a five-item lesson; an excerpt must still contain the setup, the loss,
and the reveal if the lesson asks for whole-text theme. Bank it for grades 9-10 fiction, not for the
grade 7 main-idea slot.

## Rules for adding a text

1. Verify the license before adding it.
2. Name the exact excerpt and compute FK on that excerpt.
3. Score L/I/S/K and record the highest demand.
4. Write the central idea and three to five supporting details.
5. Record the lesson slot it fits, not just the topic.
6. Add a caution line. Every text has one.
7. **One work per (category, band) cell; one passage per lesson.** A good long work is milked across
   the whole cell: take every lesson's passage from it, as long as no displayed passage repeats and
   each lesson's passage is self-sufficient. Record the cell it serves, and record how many disjoint
   passages the work can yield — it must sustain eight of 100-200 words even if only four are
   authored now.
8. **A work never serves two cells.** Not two bands, not two categories. The move from Band 5.1 to
   Band 6.1 is the transition a student always notices, so it gets fresh material.
